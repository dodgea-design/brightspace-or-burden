Learner persona

Meet Jordan

Hi, I’m Jordan.

I’m a 20-year-old college sophomore, and I use she/her pronouns. I love hiking, being outdoors, and noticing details other people miss.

I hope to become a private investigator someday. I can spot a suspicious footprint faster than I can find an assignment deadline buried halfway down a course page.

Strengths I Bring

  • Strong connections between ideas

  • Persistence and creative problem-solving
  • Attention to details others may overlook

What Helps Me Learn

  • Predictable organization
  • Clearly visible deadlines
  • A manageable first step
  • Examples of completed work
  • Progress cues and checklists
  • Approachable ways to ask for help

How My Brain Works

I have ADHD. My brain is curious, observant, and always making connections, but every notification, open tab, unfinished task, and vague direction can feel like it is competing for my attention at the same time. I can become completely absorbed in something interesting and then realize hours have passed.

Starting a task is often harder than completing it once I understand exactly where to begin. I take medication to help with focus, but I do not always like how it makes me feel, so I am not always consistent about taking it. Changes in medication or sleep can leave me feeling foggy, disconnected, and in need of time to feel grounded again.

Missing even a few classes can make returning feel overwhelming. I may not know what I missed, what matters most, or where I should restart.

Help Me Find My Way Back In

When I return after falling behind, I need the course to help me answer:

  • What is most important right now?
  • What is due next?
  • How long might it take?
  • What is the first small action?
  • How will I know when I am finished?
  • Where can I ask for help without feeling judged?

Observation or Assumption?

An observation describes something that can be directly noticed or verified. An inference is an interpretation based on those details and a person’s prior knowledge. Strong investigators separate the two so that an early assumption does not quietly become “evidence.”

Design analysis and working findings

What the Comparison Suggests

These are design-based observations from the prototype—not claims from formal research with a large participant group.

Course View A

Information is present but competes for attention. The learner must independently locate, prioritize, and sequence the steps. Returning after an absence is possible, but effortful.

Course View B

The deadline, estimated time, purpose, and first action are visible. Numbered steps guide the pathway, while response choices, success criteria, re-entry cues, and help are grouped around learner decisions.

Key Insight

Having the right information is not enough. Structure, sequencing, and clarity determine whether a learner can act on it with confidence.

Connections to Universal Design for Learning

Engagement

Predictable structure, manageable entry points, reduced uncertainty, and a clear return pathway help sustain effort without lowering the academic goal.

Representation

Headings, visual grouping, concise summaries, read-aloud support, and repeated layout cues make important information easier to perceive and interpret.

Action & Expression

Multiple response formats, visible success criteria, progress cues, and flexible pathways give learners meaningful ways to plan and demonstrate learning.

UDL interpretation: View B transfers some unnecessary executive-function demands from the learner into the design of the environment while maintaining the same learning objective.

Five Practices to Begin With

  1. Use a consistent weekly module structure.Keep names, sequence, and location predictable.
  2. Group essential orientation information.Show the purpose, exact deadline, time estimate, and first action together.
  3. Make the path visible.Use numbered, linked steps and explain what completion looks like.
  4. Design for re-entry.Tell students where to restart after an absence or interruption.
  5. Make help approachable.Provide a direct route and a message starter that reduces the burden of asking.

Reflection on Brightspace

While building and testing this prototype, I found that Brightspace offered several ways to navigate to the same information. That flexibility can be helpful, but it can also make course building and learner navigation feel less natural. The platform provides useful tools—content units, completion tracking, checklists, calendar dates, accessibility checking, Class Progress, and automated outreach—but those tools do not automatically create an executive-function-friendly course. Their value depends on whether educators select, organize, explain, and maintain them intentionally.

Working Conclusion

Brightspace can support executive functioning when educators use predictable organization, visible expectations, manageable next steps, progress cues, and clear pathways to human support. The platform alone does not create an accessible learning experience. The way its features are selected, organized, explained, and maintained determines whether the technology reduces barriers or adds new ones.
